How might we support every kind of email the organization sends, without adding a new template every time a new need shows up?

The challenge

Every email at The Nature Conservancy goes through one centralized team: a developer, a UX designer (me), and two email operations specialists. Together we support more than 100 business units.

Over time the ecosystem grew to 13 templates. Each was built for a specific need, but many overlapped, and every one needed its own maintenance, QA, documentation, and accessibility fixes. New needs usually meant another template, so every future change got more expensive.

What started as a template redesign turned out to be a systems problem.

For the team

  • Every template needed ongoing maintenance and support.
  • Updates meant duplicated development and QA.
  • Accessibility fixes had to be repeated across variations.
  • New needs added templates instead of improving the system.

For subscribers

  • Content hierarchy varied between templates.
  • Calls to action were inconsistent.
  • Mobile experiences differed by email type.
  • Accessibility wasn’t standardized, and designs didn’t draw on engagement data.

My role

I led UX strategy and design. Instead of redesigning templates one at a time, I treated the ecosystem as a system: find what could be consolidated, turn user and business insights into design principles, and design an architecture where many email types share one set of components.

  • Template and pattern audit
  • Consolidation strategy
  • Engagement analytics
  • CTA and interaction research
  • Component architecture
  • Module and template design
  • Accessibility and mobile‑first standards
  • Engineering partnership

Research

Before designing anything, I looked at how subscribers actually used our emails.

+219% Header engagement, year over year
+81% YouTube engagement, year over year
~25% Of all clicks went to external links
+22% Footer engagement

Several legacy modules were also declining. Together, these findings challenged our assumptions about how people move through an email and showed where hierarchy and navigation needed to change.

Strategy

  1. Reduce operational complexityEvery template carries a future maintenance cost. For each one, I asked whether it solved a genuinely unique need or could be covered by a more flexible pattern. The question changed from “How do we improve 13 templates?” to “How do we maintain fewer systems while supporting the same needs?”

  2. Build a reusable systemInstead of designing isolated templates, I designed reusable modules, so new needs could be met through configuration rather than new templates.

  3. Design for the long termAccessibility, responsiveness, and consistency were requirements from day one, not post‑launch fixes.

Decision 01

Give people a reason before the button

CTA research showed a clear pattern: people act when the value is explained before the call to action, not when a button is doing all the work. I moved our CTAs away from decorative containers and toward a short line of context first.

Decision 02

Make the whole thing clickable

I set clickable areas across the system. CTA modules and story cards are fully clickable, and feature stories click through from the image, the headline, and the CTA. These became base rules of the component system.

Decision 03

One component library, many email types

Newsletters, Nature News, cultivation emails, event invitations, and future campaigns all draw from the same modules: Featured Stories, Story Listings, Visual Listings, CTAs, Events, and Promotional Blocks. Every module is mobile‑first, accessible, and designed with dark mode in mind.

The solution

A consolidated email ecosystem where many communication types share one foundation, with flexibility kept where it matters most.

  • Removed redundant template variations.
  • Added new formats only where a genuine business need existed.
  • Moved shared functionality into reusable components.
  • Renamed and restructured legacy template categories to reflect their purpose.

Impact

13 → 9 Email templates
100+ Business units supported by a team of four

For the organization

  • A shared component architecture across communication types.
  • Lower long‑term maintenance by removing redundant patterns.
  • A system that can grow without template sprawl.

For subscribers

  • More consistency across every kind of email.
  • Simpler content hierarchy and CTA patterns.
  • Accessibility and mobile‑first standards everywhere.
  • Analytics guiding how templates evolve next.

Reflection

Sometimes the most impactful design work isn’t a new feature or interface. It’s a system that lets an organization work better over time.

This started as a template redesign and became an exercise in systems thinking. Focusing on scale, efficiency, and shared foundations helped both the people producing email campaigns and the people receiving them.
