What I’m doing

  1. Auditing the existing knowledge bank as a first‑time user, to find where people get lost.
  2. Recommending changes to search, navigation, and upload, including a substantially different interface where it’s warranted.
  3. Keeping SharePoint’s complexity out of the way for casual users, so adoption doesn’t stall.

Can a TNC scientist arrive, understand what this is, find the right knowledge, and contribute their own, without needing to know how SharePoint is built?

The challenge

TNC’s internal science knowledge (methods, standard operating procedures, guidance, templates, survey instruments, and media) is spread across disconnected locations. Related work is hard to discover, institutional knowledge gets lost when people move on, and teams duplicate effort.

Before I joined, the discovery team ran 35 stakeholder interviews and sent a survey to 1,100 people across TNC’s science community, then piloted two platforms before choosing SharePoint.

The goal is one authoritative internal home for finalized conservation‑science knowledge: a place where someone can find a method, understand it, and reuse it instead of rebuilding it.

It isn’t meant to replace the tools teams collaborate in day to day. Work in progress stays where it is; the knowledge bank is where approved, finished knowledge lives long term. That distinction shapes the whole experience: this shouldn’t be designed as a better file share.

Where it fits

The knowledge bank is one of several systems in TNC’s science ecosystem, each with a different job. Keeping their boundaries clear is part of the design work.

My role

The project team brought me in for UX. Platform selection, metadata, governance, and use‑case work were already moving; what was missing was someone looking at the current knowledge bank without years of learned familiarity.

I proposed starting with a functional audit centered on purpose, users, workflows, and usability, rather than visual polish. The team agreed the current interface can be challenged, and that recommending a substantially different one is in scope, as long as it can be maintained.

  • New‑user UX audit
  • Search and navigation
  • Upload and contribution
  • Information architecture
  • Metadata experience
  • Recommendations

Approach

I’m running the audit through a Six Box framework: six questions that every page, section, or site should be able to answer. Where the current knowledge bank can’t answer one clearly, that’s a gap to fix.

  1. Who is the primary audience?
  2. What job does the primary audience need done?
  3. What is the organization’s goal for this page, section, or site?
  4. How will the primary audience reach it?
  5. What messaging will be provided to the primary audience?
  6. Where will the primary audience go, or what will they do, after visiting?

First‑pass findings are in discussion with the project team.

Where it stands

  2. New‑user UX audit In progress

Why it matters

The project lists a difficult, unintuitive experience as a real risk to adoption across the organization. A knowledge bank only works if people trust it enough to look there first, and to put their own work there. That’s the problem I’m designing for.
