The real problem wasn’t the website. It was building a system that could support conservation science for years to come.

The challenge

Conservation Gateway is The Nature Conservancy’s hub for conservation science. Researchers, practitioners, policymakers, and internal teams use it to find publications, datasets, tools, and methods.

By the time I joined, it held more than 5,000 resources and years of technical debt. Some sections were maintained by individual business units; others had been neglected. There was no clear ownership model, and earlier migration attempts had failed because of the scale. Before redesigning anything, the team had to answer harder questions:

For the organization

  • Who governs the platform, and who maintains and improves it?
  • How do we migrate future sites and collections into it?
  • How do we avoid repeating past mistakes?

For users

  • Information was hard to discover, and navigation was hard to follow.
  • Taxonomies were inconsistent and search was limited.
  • Content lacked clear paths to conservation action.

My role

My title was Senior UX Designer, but because the project had stalled for years over ownership and scale, my role reached into platform strategy, stakeholder alignment, feasibility, and implementation planning.

I stayed hands‑on with the UX: information architecture, wireframes, new features and custom components, enhancements to existing AEM components, and the search and filtering experience. After the agency finished its research and recommendations, I worked with product owners, developers, and release teams to judge feasibility, set priorities, write Jira stories, and get the work into production.

As the project grew, I hired and onboarded a dedicated UX designer for the platform, then shifted into an advisor and mentor role so they could own its next chapter.

Decision 01

Make every resource structured data

Each resource gets its own structured page built on metadata: title, summary, content type, and supporting information.

That metadata is what powers aggregation, filtering, and search, and it’s what lets one resource be found from many directions.

Decision 02

Build the governance, not just the site

Earlier efforts focused on moving content. This time, governance was part of the design: who owns what, how authors publish, and how the platform is maintained after launch.

The redesign shipped with directory governance standards, authoring guidance, site governance documentation, content organization frameworks, maintenance processes, and a long‑term migration plan.

Decision 03

One platform, two very different audiences

Nature.org is built for storytelling, engagement, and fundraising. Conservation Gateway serves researchers, practitioners, and policy leaders looking for specialized resources. Both run on the same AEM platform, with the same components, developers, and release process.

For each need, I had to decide how to adapt shared components without breaking either site, and whether the value was worth the long‑term cost of maintaining something custom.

Modify an existing component

Cheapest to maintain, but every change has to work for nature.org too.

Create a new variation

Fits research use cases better, and adds one more thing to maintain.

Design around the constraint

No new build at all, when the custom version wasn’t worth the investment.

The solution

Conservation Gateway relaunched as a scalable AEM platform for conservation science, with the groundwork for more advanced discovery:

  • Improved filtering structures and taxonomy models.
  • Metadata‑driven resource organization.
  • Support for expanded search and multi‑layer filtering, with a future CloudSearch implementation.

Reflection

For most of this project, Conservation Gateway had no dedicated product team. It shared release capacity with other priorities, so every custom feature had to earn its place. Today it has its own product owner, business analyst, designer, and developers.

It taught me that good digital products aren’t shaped by user needs alone. They’re also shaped by platform constraints, shared infrastructure, available resources, and the long‑term cost of maintaining what gets built. Some of the most important decisions weren’t about what we wanted to design, but about creating the best experience within those limits while keeping a vision for growth.
